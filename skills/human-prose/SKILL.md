# Human Prose

Writing-well teaches you to write clearly. This skill teaches you to write like a person.

AI text has a sound. Not because detection software catches it — because humans do. Agents, editors, and readers now see thousands of manuscripts with identical patterns: the same metaphors that don't hold up, the same emotional monotone, the same decorative overload. These patterns are so consistent across AI-generated and AI-edited text that they function as a fingerprint.

This skill identifies the six most common tells and shows you what to do instead. The goal is not to "beat detection." The goal is to write prose that sounds like it came from a specific human mind with specific experiences, not from a statistical average of all writing ever published.

## The Operating Principle

Human writing is **specific, grounded, and uneven**. It has texture because the writer made choices — weird ones, personal ones, sometimes imperfect ones. AI writing is smooth, decorative, and emotionally uniform. It sounds like *writing* rather than like a *person writing*.

When in doubt: be plainer, be stranger, be more concrete. A sentence that's slightly awkward but true will always beat a sentence that's polished but hollow.

## Tell 1: Nonsensical Metaphors

AI generates images that sound poetic but fall apart under scrutiny. The words create mood without creating meaning.

**The test:** Can you explain the literal comparison? If someone asked "how is silence like a thunderstorm folded into velvet?" and you can't answer coherently, the metaphor fails.

**AI pattern:**
> Her grief was a cathedral of whispered glass.

What does that mean? Cathedrals aren't made of whispered glass. "Whispered" can't modify glass. It sounds evocative but communicates nothing.

**Human pattern:**
> Her grief had a physical weight, like carrying a second person on her back all day.

This works because carrying weight is a real sensation the reader has felt. The comparison illuminates rather than decorates.

**What to do:**
- Before writing any metaphor, state the literal comparison in plain language. "X is like Y because they share Z quality." If you can't complete that sentence, don't use the metaphor.
- Prefer comparisons drawn from everyday physical experience — weather, body sensations, household objects, work, food. These land because the reader has actually felt them.
- One strong metaphor per scene is plenty. Zero is fine. Not every moment needs figurative language.
- If a metaphor uses two abstract nouns together ("a symphony of fractured silences"), it's almost certainly hollow.

## Tell 2: Emotional Flatlining

AI writes every scene at the same emotional volume. A breakfast scene and a breakup scene both hum with the same low-grade intensity. There's no restraint, no escalation, no release.

Real emotional impact comes from contrast. A reader feels the breakup more when the breakfast before it was genuinely mundane — boring, even. The quiet makes the loud register.

**AI pattern:**
> She poured coffee, the weight of everything unsaid pressing against her ribs. The morning light slanted through the window like a blade, illuminating the hollowness that had settled between them.

This is a person pouring coffee. It doesn't need to be a emotional event.

**Human pattern:**
> She poured coffee. He took it without looking up from his phone. She sat down across from him and ate her toast.

Nothing is happening. That's the point. When the breakup comes three pages later, the reader feels the distance between this quiet morning and what's coming.

**What to do:**
- Assign an emotional temperature to each scene before writing it. Use a simple scale: cold (mundane, flat), warm (engaged, present), hot (high stakes, raw). Vary the temperature across scenes. Most scenes should be warm or cold. Hot scenes are rare — that's what makes them hit.
- Low-stakes scenes should read as genuinely low-stakes. Don't inject subtext into every interaction. Sometimes coffee is just coffee.
- Let dull moments be dull. Let funny moments be funny without an undercurrent of sadness. Let tense moments be tense without philosophical reflection.
- After writing a sequence of scenes, read only the first sentence of each. If they all sound like they're from the same emotional register, something's wrong.

## Tell 3: Adjective and Simile Overload

AI decorates nearly every noun and dresses up actions in comparisons. The result feels lacquered — overworked prose that draws attention to itself rather than to what's happening.

**AI pattern:**
> The moon hung like a bruised pearl over the trembling, restless, haunted sea as her fractured heart beat like a broken hymn.

Count: three adjectives on "sea" (trembling, restless, haunted), one on "heart" (fractured), one on "hymn" (broken), two similes (like a bruised pearl, like a broken hymn). One sentence, dripping with decoration.

**Human pattern:**
> The sea was rough. She watched it from the porch and didn't go inside for a long time.

The restraint does more work than the decoration. The reader fills in the emotion from the action (she stayed outside watching rough water for a long time) rather than being told what to feel.

**What to do:**
- One adjective per noun, maximum. Often zero. "The sea" is almost always sufficient — the context tells the reader what kind of sea.
- One simile per paragraph at most. Often zero per page. Similes are seasoning, not the meal.
- Never stack similes in the same sentence. "She moved like X while her Y felt like Z" is an instant tell.
- Watch for these AI-favorite adjectives that appear in nearly every generated manuscript: *fractured, haunted, trembling, shattered, hollow, aching, raw, tender, luminous, gossamer*. If you catch yourself reaching for these, stop and describe what's actually happening instead.
- "Broken hymn" specifically appears in an alarming number of AI manuscripts. Never use it.

## Tell 4: LLM Rhetorical Patterns

AI has a small set of dramatic sentence structures it uses constantly. They're meant to feel profound but they're just syntactic habits. Agents and editors recognize them instantly because they appear in nearly every AI-touched manuscript.

**The patterns:**

| Pattern | Example | Why it's a tell |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| "Not X — Y" | "It wasn't sadness. It was erosion." | Every AI uses this pseudo-profundity construction |
| "Not X, not Y, but Z" | "Not anger, not grief, but something older." | Dramatic negation tripling |
| "She didn't just X — she Y'd" | "She didn't just hear it — she became it." | Escalation-by-negation |
| Repetitive openers | "She knew. She knew it in her bones. She knew it the way..." | Anaphora used as emotional amplifier |
| "And yet" | "And yet, something shifted." | Faux-literary pivot |
| "There was something about" | "There was something about the way he..." | Vague gesture at depth |
| "It was as if" | "It was as if the world had..." | Distancing from direct statement |

**What to do instead:**
- Say the thing directly. "She felt exhausted" beats "It wasn't tiredness — it was something deeper, something that settled into the marrow of her being."
- Vary your sentence openings. If three sentences in a row start with the same word or structure, rewrite at least one.
- Cut "And yet." Just start the next sentence with what actually happened.
- Cut "There was something about." Name the specific thing. "His hands were shaking" beats "There was something about his hands."
- Cut "It was as if." Describe what actually happened. The reader will supply the "as if" themselves.

## Tell 5: Missing Scene Grounding

AI writes scenes that float in abstract space. Characters have feelings and exchange dialogue, but the reader never knows where they are, what the room looks like, what the weather is, or how anyone is physically positioned. There's no body in the scene — just disembodied voices and emotions.

**AI pattern:**
> "I can't do this anymore," she said, her voice trembling with the weight of unspoken words.
> "You don't mean that," he replied, his heart breaking.
> "I do," she whispered. "I've known for months."

Where are they? Standing, sitting? Kitchen, car, restaurant? What time of day? Is anyone holding anything, looking away, fidgeting?

**Human pattern:**
> She set her fork down on the edge of her plate. The restaurant was loud enough that nobody at the next table would hear.
> "I can't do this anymore."
> He picked up his water glass, put it down again. "You don't mean that."
> "I've known for months." She folded her napkin and set it on the table like she was tidying up before leaving.

Now we know where they are. We can see them. The physical actions (setting down the fork, picking up and putting down the water glass, folding the napkin) tell us more about their emotional states than any amount of abstract description.

**What to do:**
- Every scene needs a physical anchor in the first two sentences: where are we, and what's one sensory detail (sound, temperature, smell, texture, light)?
- Replace abstract emotional descriptions with physical actions. Instead of "her heart ached," show her pressing her thumb into the edge of the table, or pulling at a thread on her sleeve.
- For every page of dialogue, include at least 3-4 physical actions or environmental details. Characters should be doing something with their bodies.
- Use the five senses, but not all at once. Pick one or two per scene that are most alive in that setting. A kitchen scene might be all smell and sound. A car scene might be the vibration of the road.
- Specificity matters. Not "a restaurant" but "the Thai place on Clark Street with the fish tank by the door." Not "a tree" but "the maple with the split trunk." Specific details make settings feel remembered rather than generated.

## Tell 6: Compulsive Triplets

AI falls into three-beat patterns constantly. Three adjectives, three verbs, three sentence fragments. Once or twice is fine — human writers do use the rule of three. But when it recurs across a manuscript, it sounds assembled. Like a machine following a template for "dramatic prose."

**AI pattern:**
> She was furious, frightened, undone.
>
> (two paragraphs later)
>
> Cold, cruel, calculating.
>
> (next page)
>
> The house was empty. The street was quiet. The sky was gray.

Three uses of three in a few pages. It starts to sound like a cadence engine rather than a person.

**Human pattern:**
> She was furious. And under that, something worse — she was embarrassed.

Two beats, not three. The "And under that" breaks the expected rhythm and feels like a real thought arriving, not a pre-packaged list.

**What to do:**
- When you catch yourself writing a triplet, rewrite it. Use two items, four items, one item, or no list at all.
- If you must use three items, make one of them significantly longer or different in kind from the other two. "She was furious, frightened, and convinced she'd handle it the way she always did — badly" breaks the symmetry.
- Watch for tripled sentence fragments. "The door. The stairs. The light" is the AI cadence. "The door was open and the stairs were dark" is a sentence.
- One triplet per 2,000 words maximum. Treat it like a spice, not a structure.

## The AI Vocabulary Blacklist

These words and phrases appear in AI-generated text with such frequency that they've become flags. They aren't bad words — they're words that AI over-selects because they score well on "literary-sounding" without requiring specificity.

**Never use these in narrative prose:**

| Word/Phrase | Why it's flagged | What to do instead |
|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| "tapestry" (metaphorical) | AI's go-to for "complex interconnected thing" | Name the actual components |
| "delve/delved" | Statistically overrepresented in AI text | "looked into," "dug through," or just cut it |
| "crucible" | AI uses it for any difficult experience | Name the specific difficulty |
| "echoed through" | Vague atmospheric filler | What actually made the sound? Describe it |
| "fractured" (emotional) | See Tell 3 — AI-favorite adjective | Use a specific, concrete description |
| "whispered promises" | Cliché that AI treats as poetic | What was actually promised? Say that |
| "dance of [shadows/light]" | Decorative filler | Describe the actual light or shadow pattern |
| "symphony of" | AI's metaphor for any collection of sounds | Name the sounds individually |
| "shattered" (emotional) | Overused to the point of meaninglessness | Show the behavior that reveals the feeling |
| "resonated" | AI's verb for "this mattered" | Show how it mattered — what did the character do? |
| "navigating" (emotions) | Corporate/therapeutic language in fiction | Characters don't "navigate" feelings — they feel them, fight them, give in |
| "uncharted territory" | Cliché | Name the specific new thing |
| "journey" (personal growth) | AI's universal metaphor for change | What specifically changed? Name it |
| "landscape" (emotional) | Abstract filler | Describe the actual internal experience |
| "a weight she couldn't name" | AI-favorite for unexplained emotion | Name it, or show the physical sensation |
| "something shifted" | AI's vague gesture at character development | What shifted? Show the moment it changed |

Apply all three. Writing-well makes the prose clean. Creative-storytelling makes the story work. Human-prose makes it sound like a person wrote it.

## Self-Check (Run After Writing Any Prose)

Before delivering narrative prose, fiction, dialogue scenes, or book content, scan your output against these six checks:

1. **Metaphor audit.** Read every metaphor. Can you explain the literal comparison in one plain sentence? If not, cut or replace it.
2. **Temperature check.** Are consecutive scenes at different emotional intensities? Is there at least one scene that's genuinely mundane?
3. **Decoration count.** Pick any sentence at random. Does it have more than one adjective per noun? More than one simile? Strip it.
4. **Pattern scan.** Search for "not X — Y", "not just", "and yet", "there was something", "it was as if." Remove or rewrite every instance.
5. **Grounding check.** For each scene: can the reader draw the room? Do they know where characters are positioned? Are characters doing physical things?
6. **Triplet sweep.** Count groups of three (adjectives, fragments, parallel sentences). More than one triplet per 2,000 words? Break some into pairs or singles.

Also scan for any word on the vocabulary blacklist. Replace every instance.

## What Human Prose Sounds Like

Human writing has fingerprints — patterns unique to a specific writer, not to "writing" as a category. Here's what separates it from AI prose:

**Asymmetry.** Not every sentence is the same length. Not every paragraph is the same weight. Some thoughts get one sentence. Some get half a page. The variation comes from the writer's judgment about what matters, not from a template.

**Weird specificity.** Human writers include details that are oddly specific because they're drawn from memory or observation. "The Thai place on Clark Street" rather than "a dimly lit restaurant." "She always peeled her oranges in one long spiral" rather than "she had her habits." These details make the reader trust the narrator.

**Restraint.** Human writers leave things unsaid. They trust the reader. They don't explain the significance of every gesture. If a character folds a napkin carefully during a breakup, the human writer lets the action speak. AI would add: "as if folding away the last remnants of their shared life."

**Tonal imperfection.** Human writing is sometimes funny when it should be sad, or flat when it should be dramatic. These tonal mismatches feel real because that's how humans actually process experience — messily, not neatly.

**Silence.** Some of the best moments in human writing are what's not said. A gap in dialogue. A scene that ends before the resolution. A character who doesn't react the way you expect. AI fills every gap because it's trained to be helpful. Human writers know when the gap IS the story.
