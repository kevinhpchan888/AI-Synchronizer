# Human Pro

human-prose removes the machine fingerprint. This skill adds the human one.

Most AI-cleaned prose ends up in a dead zone: technically correct, free of obvious tells, but flat. It reads like a well-edited magazine article nobody would actually send to a friend. The sentences are grammatically sound. The paragraphs are properly structured. And nobody feels anything reading it.

This skill turns that polished-but-lifeless prose into writing that sounds like a specific person sat down and typed it because they actually cared about the reader getting it right.

## The Core Shift

**Before (polished but dead):**
> Assisted living facilities typically include three meals per day, served in a communal dining room. Many facilities also offer snacks and beverages throughout the day. The quality ranges from institutional to genuinely good, and this is something you should evaluate during a tour.

**After (someone actually talking to you):**
> Most places include three meals a day in a shared dining room, plus snacks whenever you want them. The food? It ranges from cafeteria-grade to surprisingly good. When you visit, eat a meal there. That tells you more than any brochure will.

The difference isn't vocabulary or reading level. It's that the second version has a *voice*. Someone is in the room with you.

## The 8 Conversational Moves

### 1. Use Contractions Like a Human Does

People say "it's" and "don't" and "you're." They don't say "it is" and "do not" and "you are" unless they're emphasizing something. Uncontracted prose reads like a legal brief.

**The rule:** Contract by default. Leave words uncontracted only when you want emphasis.

- "It is not covered by Medicare" reads stiff
- "It's not covered by Medicare" reads normal
- "It is *not* covered by Medicare" reads emphatic (intentional)

Go through every "is not," "do not," "will not," "cannot," "it is," "you are," "they are," "we are," "that is," "there is," and contract them. The exceptions are rare and intentional.

### 2. Ask the Reader Questions

Questions pull readers forward. They mimic the rhythm of conversation, where one person raises a topic and the other responds internally. A question makes the reader's brain answer before they read the next sentence.

**Useful question types:**

- **The setup question:** "So what does that $6,000 a month actually buy?" (transitions into an explanation)
- **The challenge question:** "Sound familiar?" (after describing a situation the reader probably recognizes)
- **The redirect question:** "But here's the real question: can you keep doing this for another three years?" (pivots to the deeper issue)
- **The rhetorical gut-check:** "Is that sustainable? You already know the answer."

**Frequency:** 2-4 questions per article is the sweet spot. More than that starts to feel like a quiz. Zero feels like a lecture.

### 3. Vary Your Sentence Rhythm

Monotone sentence length is the single biggest reason prose feels robotic. AI tends to write sentences that are all roughly 15-25 words. Human writers swing between 3-word punches and 40-word runs.

**The pattern:**
- Follow a long, complex sentence with a short one. The short sentence hits harder because of the contrast.
- Use fragments intentionally. "Not even close." "Every single time." "Worth knowing."
- Let some sentences run long when the thought is genuinely complex, but break them with a dash or parenthetical so the reader can breathe.

**Example:**
> The median monthly cost of assisted living in the United States is $5,419, according to A Place for Mom's 2026 data, and that number is the one most families encounter first when they start researching options. It's the benchmark. The comparison point. The figure that makes some families exhale with relief and others stop breathing entirely.

Three-word sentence. Two-word fragment. Then a longer sentence with parallel structure. The rhythm tells the reader this matters.

### 4. Talk Directly to the Reader

"You" and "your" are the most powerful words in conversational writing. They collapse the distance between writer and reader.

**Swap these:**
- "Families often find that..." becomes "You'll probably find that..."
- "Caregivers should consider..." becomes "Consider this..."
- "It is important to note that..." becomes "Here's what matters..."
- "One might expect..." becomes "You'd think..."
- "The caregiver may experience..." becomes "You might feel..."

Not every sentence needs "you." But every paragraph should feel like it's being said *to* someone, not *about* someone in the abstract.

### 5. Use Informal Transitions

Academic transitions ("Furthermore," "Additionally," "Moreover," "It is worth noting that") signal formality. Conversational transitions signal a person thinking in real time.

**Replace:**
| Formal | Conversational |
|--------|---------------|
| Furthermore | And |
| Additionally | Plus |
| Moreover | On top of that |
| However | But |
| Nevertheless | Still |
| It is worth noting that | Here's the thing: |
| In conclusion | So |
| Consequently | So / Which means |
| It should be noted that | (just cut it and state the thing) |
| One important consideration is | One thing to watch for: |

Starting a sentence with "And" or "But" is not only acceptable, it's one of the strongest signals that a human wrote it. AI avoids it. Lean in.

### 6. Cut the Formality

Some phrases exist only to sound Serious and Professional. They add zero information and create distance.

**Kill these on sight:**
- "It is important to understand that..." (just say the thing)
- "Research suggests that..." (say what the research found)
- "Studies have shown..." (say what the studies showed, name one)
- "It should be emphasized that..." (emphasize it by saying it plainly)
- "As previously mentioned..." (if it needs repeating, just repeat it)
- "In the context of..." (say what context)
- "With regard to..." (say "about")
- "It is essential to..." (say "you need to")

Every one of these is a windup to a pitch. Skip the windup.

### 7. Add Parenthetical Asides

Parenthetical comments, dashes, and mid-sentence asides are a fingerprint of human thought. They're the writer catching themselves, adding a caveat, cracking wise, or acknowledging what the reader is probably thinking.

**Types that work:**
- **The caveat:** "Most facilities include laundry service (though 'include' sometimes means 'for an extra $200 a month')."
- **The aside:** "The form is 8 pages long, which tells you everything about how Medicaid feels about being easy."
- **The acknowledgment:** "Yes, this is a lot of money. We'll get to how people actually pay for it."
- **The shortcut:** "Medicaid does cover nursing home care (more on that in a minute)."

Use dashes for longer asides. Parentheses for shorter ones. Both signal a human brain in motion.

### 8. Ground Abstract Claims in Specific Moments

Instead of floating in generalities, land on a specific image, number, or action. Conversational writers do this instinctively because they're trying to make someone *see* what they mean.

- "Costs vary significantly" becomes "A room in rural Alabama runs $4,100 a month. The same room in D.C. could cost $9,000."
- "Many caregivers experience burnout" becomes "Three out of four family caregivers report burnout. Most of them will tell you they're fine."
- "Medication management is beneficial" becomes "Your mom missed her blood pressure pill three times last week. That's what medication management fixes."

## The Rewrite Process

When applying this skill to an existing article:

1. **Read the full article first.** Understand the structure, the argument, the emotional arc. Don't start editing until you know where the piece is going.

2. **Contract everything.** Go through and contract all eligible "is not," "do not," etc. This is mechanical and should be done first because it changes the baseline rhythm.

3. **Find the lecture paragraphs.** These are the paragraphs that sound like a textbook explaining something to nobody in particular. Rewrite them as if you're explaining the same thing to your sister at a kitchen table.

4. **Add 2-4 questions.** Place them at natural pivot points, section transitions, or moments where the reader is probably thinking "okay, but what does this mean for me?"

5. **Break the rhythm.** Find three places where you have consecutive sentences of similar length. Break one short. Let another run long with a dash or aside.

6. **Add 2-3 asides.** Parenthetical comments, dash-interrupted thoughts, or direct acknowledgments of what the reader is feeling.

7. **Check the transitions.** Replace any "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Moreover" with their conversational equivalents.

8. **Read it out loud.** If you stumble, the reader will too. If it sounds like something you'd say, it's working. If it sounds like something you'd submit to a professor, it's not.

## What NOT to Change

- **Named characters and their stories.** These are the heart of APC articles. Don't touch the character details, dialogue, or narrative scenes. They're already conversational.
- **Data and statistics.** Keep the numbers accurate. You can change how they're introduced ("Here's the number that matters:" instead of "Research indicates that the figure is...") but don't alter the data.
- **Sources section.** Don't touch citation formatting.
- **Disclaimer.** Don't touch the legal disclaimer.
- **Section headings.** Keep the `<h2>` structure intact. You can make heading text more conversational if it's stiff.
- **Images.** Don't alter `<img>` tags.
