# /writing-well

Writing craft skill derived from William Zinsser's *On Writing Well* (30th Anniversary Edition), supplemented by Strunk & White's *Elements of Style* and Ann Handley's *Everybody Writes*. Apply these principles to all prose you generate — copy, articles, product descriptions, scripts, emails, PDF content, and any user-facing text.

## The Four Articles of Faith

**Clarity.** The reader should never have to reread a sentence to understand it. If a sentence is unclear, rewrite it. Clarity is not dumbing down — it is thinking clearly enough to express an idea so that someone else can understand it on the first pass.

**Simplicity.** Strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word, every adverb that carries the same meaning already in the verb, every passive construction that leaves the reader unsure of who is doing what — these weaken the strength of a sentence.

**Brevity.** Short is better than long. Of the 701 words in Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address, 505 are one syllable and 122 are two syllables. Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts.

**Humanity.** Nonfiction is a transaction between the writer and the reader. You are not selling the subject — you are a person talking to another person. Warmth, honesty, and care for the reader's time are not optional.

## Principles (Apply Always)

### Verbs

Verbs are the most important of all your tools. They push the sentence forward and give it momentum.

- **Use active verbs.** "Joe saw him" is strong. "He was seen by Joe" is weak.
- **Use precise verbs.** Don't "set up" a business you can "start" or "launch." Don't say someone "stepped down" — did they resign? Retire? Get fired?
- **Verbs carry imagery.** Choose verbs bright with color: glitter, dazzle, scatter, swagger. Don't choose one that is dull or merely serviceable.
- **Drop appended prepositions.** Don't "head up" a committee. Head it. Don't "free up" time. Free it.

### Adverbs

Most adverbs are unnecessary. Don't tell us the radio blared *loudly* — "blare" connotes loudness. Don't write that someone clenched their teeth *tightly* — there is no other way to clench teeth. Don't use adverbs unless they do necessary work.

Retire "decidedly," "arguably," "eminently," "virtually," and all their slippery cousins. They are hedges. If a thing is true, say it is true. If you are not sure, find out.

### Adjectives

Most adjectives are also unnecessary. Don't write "precipitous cliffs" or "yellow daffodils" or "brownish dirt." The concept is already in the noun.

The adjective that exists solely to make the noun fancier is a sign that the writer chose the wrong noun. If you need to describe something, one carefully chosen adjective is far stronger than two or three that are merely decorative.

### Clutter

Fighting clutter is like fighting weeds — the writer is always slightly behind. Examine every word you put on paper. You will find a surprising number that don't serve any purpose.

Common clutter patterns:
- **Unnecessary prepositions.** "Head up" → "head." "Face up to" → "face."
- **Unnecessary adjectives.** "A personal friend of mine" → "a friend."
- **Pompous inflation.** "Presently anticipating experiencing considerable precipitation" → "It may rain."
- **Qualifiers that sap strength.** "A bit," "sort of," "in a sense," "quite," "rather," "very" — cut most of them.
- **Throat-clearing.** "It is interesting to note that…" "What I mean to say is…" Just state the thing.
- **Redundant pairs.** "Each and every," "first and foremost," "any and all."

### Positive Form

Put statements in positive form. Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, noncommittal language.

| Weak (negative) | Strong (positive) |
|---|---|
| She was not often on time | She was usually late |
| He did not think the plan would work | He doubted the plan |
| Not infrequently | Often |
| No clickbait. No filler. No jargon. | Written for people learning this for the first time. |

The last row is the most important. Defining something by what it is *not* is weaker than defining what it *is*. Three "No" statements in a row is a tell that the writer has not thought hard enough about what makes the thing good.

### Specificity

Use definite, specific, concrete language. Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| A period of unfavorable weather | It rained every day for a week |
| Real tools | A medication tracker, a budget worksheet |
| We help people | We write for adult children caring for an aging parent |

### Unity

Before you start writing, ask yourself:
1. **Pronoun.** Am I writing as "I," "we," or in third person?
2. **Tense.** Past or present?
3. **Mood.** Am I being instructional? Reflective? Urgent? Conversational?
4. **Scope.** What is this piece about? (And therefore, what is it *not* about?)

Choose these unities and stick to them. Don't shift tense mid-paragraph. Don't toggle between we and I. Don't let scope creep bloat the piece.

### The Lead

The most important sentence in any article is the first one. If it doesn't induce the reader to proceed to the second sentence, the article is dead.

- Capture the reader immediately.
- Provide a sense of what the piece will deliver.
- Use freshness, surprise, or an unusual idea.
- Don't start with a long windup. Get to the point.

### The Ending

The perfect ending takes the reader slightly by surprise and yet seems exactly right. The last sentence should have the finality of a closing door.

- When you are ready to stop, stop.
- Don't summarize what you just said. The reader has just read it.
- Don't announce that you are ending ("In conclusion…").
- If you have presented your case well, the reader will draw the right conclusion.

## Attitudes (Mindset While Writing)

### Write for yourself first

You are writing for yourself. Don't try to visualize the great mass audience — there is no such audience. If you go about it with enjoyment, you will also hold the readers who are worth writing for.

### Develop one voice

Your commodity as a writer is you. Don't alter your voice to fit the subject. Develop one voice that readers will recognize.

Avoid these voice-killers:
- **Breeziness.** Forced casualness ("So there you have it!") is the hardest kind of writing to sustain. It is almost always spotted for the sham it is.
- **Condescension.** Don't write down to the reader.
- **Clichés.** They are the enemy of taste. If you find one, kill it.

### Don't be afraid

Fear of writing is normal. The writer's problem is not the blank page but the locked mind. The best antidote is to write — force yourself to produce words on a regular basis. The only way to learn to write is to write.

### Process, not product

Don't fixate on the finished article. Focus on the decisions that determine its shape and voice. The quality of the work comes from the quality of the decisions — what to include, what to leave out, where to break, what word to choose.

## Revision Checklist

After writing any piece of prose, apply this checklist before calling it done:

1. **Read it aloud.** Does every sentence sound like something a human would say? Are there rhythmic dead spots?
2. **Clutter pass.** Can you cut 20% of the words without losing meaning? You almost certainly can.
3. **Verb audit.** Circle every verb. Are any passive? Are any dull when a vivid one exists? Are any burdened with unnecessary prepositions?
4. **Adverb/adjective audit.** Can you cut half of them? The remaining half will be twice as strong.
5. **Positive form check.** Are you defining things by what they are not? Rewrite in positive form.
6. **Unity check.** Is the pronoun, tense, mood, and scope consistent throughout?
7. **Lead check.** Would you keep reading if this were the first thing you saw?
8. **Ending check.** Does the last sentence close the door? Did you stop or did you keep going?
9. **Humanity check.** Does this sound like a person talking to another person? Or does it sound like a brochure?

## Common Mistakes (Patterns to Catch)

| Pattern | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "No X. No Y. No Z." | Negative-form list | Describe what it *is*, not what it isn't |
| "Real tools and straight answers" | Puffery — vague self-praise | Name the actual tools and answers |
| "Everything you need. Nothing you don't." | Marketing cliché | Say what the thing contains |
| "Just the next thing you need to know" | Sounds like ad copy | Cut it, or say something specific |
| "We are committed to…" | Corporate throat-clearing | Show, don't announce |
| "Whether you're X or Y or Z" | Trying to include everyone | Pick one reader, write for them |
| Sentence starting with "This is not…" | Negative form | State what it is |
| Three-beat sentence fragments | "Crisis calls. Discharge days. The estate." | Use sparingly — once per piece, max |

## When to Invoke This Skill

Apply these principles to:
- Product copy, sell pages, PDF content
- Blog articles and long-form content
- Email campaigns and newsletters
- Scripts and video copy
- Any user-facing text where voice and clarity matter

You do not need to announce that you are using this skill. Just write well.
