## Step 1: Outline (mandatory, before any prose)

### Top-down: the one-paragraph book

Before any chapter, write a 150-word paragraph that IS the book. What the reader learns, in what order, with what payoff. If the paragraph is boring, the book will be boring.

### Structure frameworks (pick one based on book type)

**Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS)** — for authority books positioning Kevin as the guide:
1. Open on reader's pain (chapter 1)
2. Agitate: why the pain persists, why common solutions fail (chapters 2-3)
3. Introduce Kevin's framework (chapter 4)
4. Walk through the framework (chapters 5-N)
5. Case studies / proof (chapters N+1, N+2)
6. Execution playbook (chapter N+3)
7. What to do Monday morning (final chapter)

**StoryBrand / Hero's Journey adapted** — for narrative-driven authority:
1. A character (reader) who wants something
2. Has a problem
3. Meets a guide (Kevin)
4. Who gives them a plan
5. And calls them to action
6. That ends in success / avoids failure

**The 5-Pillar Framework** — for KDP info products where "content density" sells:
- 5 or 7 core principles, each a chapter
- Each chapter: principle, why it works, how to apply, example, exercise
- Works because it scans as "complete" and review-friendly

**The Case-Study Spine** — for operator-credibility books:
- 8-12 case studies, each a chapter, each extracting one lesson
- Intro sets the frame, outro synthesizes across cases

Choose one. Do not hybrid. Hybrids confuse readers and slow writing.

### Chapter architecture (every chapter, same skeleton)

1. **Hook** (100-300 words): a scene, a question, a surprising fact, a reader-mind-reading opener
2. **Promise** (50-100 words): what the reader will be able to do by the end of this chapter
3. **Body** (2000-4000 words): the content, in 3-5 sections with H2/H3 subheads
4. **Payoff** (200-400 words): the big idea distilled, the "aha" moment
5. **Application** (100-300 words): one concrete action or question the reader carries out

Total: 2500-5000 words per chapter. 15-25 chapters.

## Step 2: Voice development

### Kevin's voice (baseline constraints)

- **Singapore operator** perspective. Not American hustle-culture. Not British reserved. Specifically SG: pragmatic, numbers-first, globally aware, mildly dry.
- **Direct, not combative.** Tells the reader hard truths but does not beat them up.
- **Numbers-backed.** Every major claim has a number, a date, or a case.
- **No em-dashes.** Use commas, periods, or parentheses.
- **No "let's dive in" energy.** No corporate-LinkedIn-hustler patterns.
- **Contractions OK.** "It's", "you're", "we'll" — human voice, not press release.

### Voice calibration exercise (run before chapter 1)

Kevin writes 500 words on a trivial topic (his morning coffee, his commute, anything) and reads it aloud. If he sounds like himself on a WhatsApp voice note, voice is right. If he sounds like a corporate memo or a ChatGPT summary, voice is broken and needs to be re-anchored.

### Voice tells to kill

Replace every instance of these in every draft:

| Kill this | Because |
|---|---|
| "In today's fast-paced world" | LinkedIn opener, zero signal |
| "It's important to note that" | Throat-clearing, delete the clause |
| "Dive into" | Dead metaphor |
| "Game-changer" | Cliché |
| "Leverage" as a verb | Jargon |
| "Synergy" | Jargon |
| "In conclusion" | Reader knows the chapter is ending |
| "As we discussed" | Recap bloat |
| "It goes without saying" | Then don't say it |
| Em-dashes | Kevin's hard rule |
| "This is a game-changer for..." | Double offense |
| "Unlock" your X | Marketing-speak |

### AI-slop detection (every draft, before Kevin submits)

AI-generated text has tells. Kill them:

- **Tricolon explosion**: three parallel clauses for every point. "It's practical, powerful, and transformative." Break the pattern.
- **Safety hedging**: "It's important to consider that different approaches may work better for different situations." Delete. Be specific.
- **Generic enthusiasm**: "This is a fascinating approach that many find helpful." Cut.
- **Empty transitions**: "Moreover, furthermore, additionally, consequently." Use one. Prefer none.
- **List addiction**: bulleting everything. Some sections should be prose.
- **Hallucinated specificity**: "Studies show that 73% of..." Verify or delete.
- **The "not only...but also" tic.** Overused. Rewrite.

If a paragraph could have been written by any business book author, it is slop. Rewrite with Kevin's specific voice and specific data.

## Step 3: Copywriting doctrine embedded in prose

Even in a nonfiction book, every chapter is a micro-sales-page for the NEXT chapter. Apply copy doctrine to keep readers reading.

### Sugarman's Slippery Slope

Every sentence's only job: make the reader read the next sentence. Short sentences. Curiosity gaps. Cliffhangers between sections. Open loops closed later in the chapter.

### Cialdini's 6 (use sparingly, not all at once)

- **Reciprocity**: give the reader something useful early (a framework, a checklist) before asking for their attention
- **Commitment**: ask them to do a small exercise early; they will finish the book to stay consistent
- **Social proof**: case studies, quotes, numbers
- **Authority**: Kevin's credentials once, early, then let the work speak
- **Liking**: self-deprecation when honest, not performative
- **Scarcity**: "most operators do not see this" — earned, not manufactured

### Cattoni's Emotional-Rational-Urgency

Every big-claim paragraph:
1. **Emotional hook**: what the reader feels right now
2. **Rational backing**: the data, the logic, the source
3. **Urgency anchor**: why this matters today, not next quarter

### Joanna Wiebe's Jobs-to-Be-Done

Every chapter opens with "the reader hired this book to [job]". If a section does not advance that job, cut it.

### Stefan Georgi's RMBC (Research-Mechanism-Big idea-Close)

For the book's overall structure:
- **Research** chapters (1-2): establish the problem is real and widespread
- **Mechanism** chapters (3-N-3): reveal WHY the problem exists (this is Kevin's wedge)
- **Big idea** chapters (N-2 to N-1): the specific method or framework
- **Close**: the playbook for action

## Step 4: Draft cycles

### Cycle 1: Zero draft (ugly, 100% done)

- Target: 80% of word count goal
- No editing, no fact-checking, no polishing
- Write every chapter end-to-end in one sitting if possible
- If Kevin hits a research gap mid-draft, mark it `[TK: verify X]` and keep writing
- Timebox: 4-8 weeks for 40k words at 1-2k words/day

### Cycle 2: Structural edit

- Read the whole manuscript in one sitting (1-2 days, printed or on Kindle)
- Ask of every chapter:
  - Does it earn its place?
  - Does it move the book forward or repeat?
  - Does its promise get paid off?
- Cut chapters that fail. Merge thin chapters. Expand under-served chapters.
- Reorder if the flow is wrong. This is the one time reordering is allowed.
- Timebox: 1-2 weeks

### Cycle 3: Line edit

- Chapter by chapter, tighten every paragraph
- Kill weak verbs (`is/was/had` — use active verbs where possible)
- Vary sentence length (if every sentence is the same length, readers disengage)
- Apply the voice-tells kill list aggressively
- Read every chapter aloud — if a sentence is awkward in the mouth, rewrite
- Timebox: 2-3 weeks

### Cycle 4: Copy edit + proofread

- Professional copy editor ($500-2000 for a 40k book, Reedsy Marketplace or upwork vetted)
- Line-level grammar, consistency, style sheet adherence
- THEN proofread (different person, different pass) for typos
- Do not skip either. KDP reviews are brutal on typos.

### Cycle 5 (optional): Beta readers

- 5-8 readers from the target persona (not friends, not family)
- Give them a PDF + a 10-question feedback form (what was confusing, what to cut, what to add, would you recommend it, etc.)
- Timebox: 2 weeks out, integrate over 1 week

Total timeline for a 40k book: 12-20 weeks of focused work (not elapsed weeks). Be honest with Kevin about this.

Create `style-sheet.md` with Kevin's consistent choices:

## Step 6: Pacing and chapter rhythm

### Every chapter has variable internal pacing:

- Open fast (hook in first 2 sentences)
- Slow in middle (develop the idea, data, examples)
- Accelerate at end (payoff + application)

### Across the book:

- Chapters alternate between conceptual and applied. Don't stack three theory chapters in a row.
- Chapter length variance is OK but with purpose. Don't have a 3000-word chapter next to an 800-word chapter without justification.
- Place your strongest chapter at 60-70% of the book (not the middle, not the end). That's where engagement sags on long reads.

### Scene breaks and visual variety

- Use `***` or similar scene breaks within long chapters
- One diagram or table per 2-3 chapters, NOT every chapter (over-visualizing signals "textbook")
- Bullet lists used purposefully, not as a crutch
- Pull quotes (highlighted passages) once per chapter maximum

## Step 7: Things to refuse

- **"Can you just write it for me with AI?"** Refuse. The book will be slop, readers will detect it, reviews will tank. Kevin can draft WITH AI assistance (outline, brainstorming, first-pass sentence shaping) but the final voice must be his, or it is not an authority book.
- **"I don't need beta readers."** Pushback. 5 beta readers catch 10 structural problems.
- **"I'll skip the copy edit to save money."** Refuse. Self-published books with typos on page 5 earn 1-star reviews that kill rank.
- **"Shorter is better, let's do 15k words."** If Kevin is writing B+C, that's a lead-magnet not an authority book. Hold the line at 35k minimum.
- **"Let's add 10 more chapters for completeness."** Refuse bloat. Kill a chapter for every chapter added.
- **"This AI passage reads fine."** If it reads fine to Kevin, it reads like every other book to the reader. Make it specifically Kevin's.
